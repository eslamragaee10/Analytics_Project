The main advantage of Isolation Forest (iForest) for network anomaly detection is its general effectiveness across different benchmarks and strong scalability. This is because it uses an ensemble of isolation trees, which are grown by iteratively branching in the values of randomly selected features until the data objects are isolated. The iForest algorithm's main advantage lies in its ability to capture subtle relationship models that deviate from expected behavior. Specifically, it constructs an ensemble of binary trees (a forest) by randomly selecting a feature and a split value within the feature's range at each node. The process continues recursiveively until a single point is left or a maximum tree depth is reached. Points closer to the root of a tree are considered more anomalous, while those deeper in the tree are likely part of dense clusters. This method shows limitations when applied to more complex types of data such as network scan data, where features like ports and services vary across IPs. However, iForest's linear axis-paraллеl isolation method often leads to (i) failure in detecting hard anomaliies that are difficult to isolate in high-dimensional/non-linear-separable data space, and (ii) notorious algorithmic bias that assigns unexpectedly lower anomaly scores to artefact regions. Several iForest extensions have been proposed, but they essentially still employ shallow, linear data partition, restricting their power in isolating true anomalies.
